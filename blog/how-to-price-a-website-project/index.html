How to price a website project

“How much for a website?” has no single answer — but there is a reliable way to work out yours. Here's the method, with a worked example.

Key points
  • Break the website into parts and estimate hours for each.
  • Multiply by your rate, then add a revision buffer and, on a platform, the platform fee.
  • Keep client costs — domain, hosting, premium plugins — separate from your fee.
  • Offer maintenance as a monthly service for recurring income.

Step 1: Ask the right questions

Before any number, understand the project. A short list of questions saves hours later:

  • How many pages, and which ones?
  • Who provides the text and images?
  • Any features: contact forms, booking, online payments, a shop, multiple languages?
  • Platform preference: WordPress, Shopify, custom code?
  • Deadline, and who gives feedback?
  • Do they already have a domain, hosting and branding?

Step 2: Break it into parts and estimate hours

Estimate each part separately. Your own past projects are the best guide; if you don't track hours yet, start now.

PartHours (example)
Discovery and planning3
Design (home + inner page templates)10
Build five pages16
Contact form and integrations3
Mobile testing and speed4
Launch and handover2
Total38

Step 3: Apply your rate

Use a rate that covers your income goal, costs and unbilled time — not a guess. If you don't know it, calculate it first.

Step 4: Add buffer and fees

Projects almost always take longer than planned. Add a revision buffer of 10–20%. If you sell through Fiverr or Upwork, gross up the total so the platform's cut doesn't come out of your pocket.

Quote = hours × rate × (1 + buffer) ÷ (1 − platform fee)

For the example: 38 hours × $25 = $950. Add a 15% buffer: $1,092.50. On Fiverr (20%): $1,092.50 ÷ 0.8 ≈ $1,366.

Step 5: Separate client costs

Domains, hosting, premium themes and plugins, paid fonts and stock photos belong to the client. List them separately — either the client buys them directly, or you buy them and pass on the cost. Keeping them out of your fee keeps your price comparable and avoids paying for renewals yourself.

Step 6: Offer packages

Giving two or three options lets the client choose instead of haggling:

  • Starter — fewer pages, template-based design.
  • Business — your main offer, custom design, the common features.
  • Premium — extra features, faster delivery, training session.

Step 7: Add maintenance as recurring income

After launch, many clients need updates, backups, security checks and small edits. A monthly maintenance plan with a clear hours limit gives them peace of mind and gives you predictable income.

Put it in writing

Send a quotation listing pages, features, revisions, timeline and payment terms (for example 50% upfront), and agree the scope before starting. Our guide to stopping scope creep shows what to include.

Frequently asked questions

How do freelancers price a website?

Break it into parts, estimate hours for each, multiply by your rate, add a revision buffer and any platform fee, and list client costs like hosting separately.

Should hosting and domain be included in my price?

Usually keep them separate. They're the client's ongoing costs and renew every year.

Should I charge hourly or fixed price for a website?

Most clients prefer fixed price for a clearly defined site. Use hourly for open-ended changes and ongoing work.

Fees, limits and rules change. We check them regularly and date every update, but always confirm the final number with the platform, your provider or an adviser before you rely on it.

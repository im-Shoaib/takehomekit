Scope creep: how to spot it and stop it

“Just one small change” is how a $300 project turns into $300 for twice the work. Here's how to set limits up front and handle extra requests without awkwardness.

Key points
  • Scope creep is work added after the price was agreed, without more time or money.
  • Prevent it with a written scope: deliverables, revision rounds and a rate for extras.
  • When a new request arrives, name it, price it, and let the client choose.
  • Fixed-price projects are most at risk — know your break-even hours.

What scope creep looks like

  • “Can you also add a blog page?” on a five-page website.
  • A fourth and fifth round of revisions on a logo quoted with two.
  • Feedback that changes the brief: “Actually, we want it to look completely different.”
  • “While you're at it, can you set up our email too?”

None of these requests is unreasonable. The problem is doing them for free, quietly, until the project costs you more than it pays.

Why it hurts most on fixed-price work

On hourly contracts, extra work means extra pay. On fixed price, every extra hour lowers your effective rate. Know your break-even point — the number of hours at which the fixed price stops being better than billing hourly.

Prevent it before you start

Most scope creep is prevented in writing, before work begins. Your quote or agreement should cover:

  1. Deliverables — a specific list. “Five pages: Home, About, Services, Blog, Contact.”
  2. What's not included — “Copywriting, hosting and email setup are not included.”
  3. Revision rounds — “Two rounds of revisions on the agreed design.”
  4. Extra work — “Work outside this scope is quoted separately at $X per hour and starts after written approval.”
  5. Feedback timing — “Delays in feedback move the delivery date by the same amount.”

Contract wording you can use

The fee covers the deliverables listed above and [two] rounds of revisions.
Anything not listed — including new pages, features or a change of direction — is outside the scope.
Extra work will be quoted separately at [rate] per hour and will only begin after written approval.

How to handle a new request

Use a simple three-step reply: acknowledge, name it, offer a choice.

Happy to help with that! Adding [request] is outside our original scope, so here are two options:

1. I add it now for [price], and delivery moves to [date].
2. We finish the current scope first and handle this as a follow-up project.

Which would you prefer?
  • Stay friendly. The client usually doesn't realise it's extra.
  • Be specific about price and time — vague answers invite “so you'll do it for free?”
  • Get approval in writing before starting the extra work.

Small favours: when it's fine

A five-minute fix for a good client can be worth doing free — it builds goodwill. The rule: make it a conscious choice, and say so (“I've included this as a small extra at no charge”). That keeps it a favour, not a new expectation.

On Fiverr and Upwork

  • Fiverr: handle extra requests with a custom offer or gig extras, so the work and payment stay on the platform.
  • Upwork: add a new milestone for fixed-price contracts, or agree extra hours on hourly ones.

Frequently asked questions

What is scope creep?

Extra work added to a project after the price and deliverables were agreed, without extra time or payment.

How do I stop scope creep without upsetting the client?

Acknowledge the request, explain it's outside the agreed scope, and offer a choice: add it for a price and new date, or do it later as a separate project.

What should a contract include to prevent scope creep?

A specific list of deliverables, what's excluded, the number of revision rounds and a rate for extra work.

Fees, limits and rules change. We check them regularly and date every update, but always confirm the final number with the platform, your provider or an adviser before you rely on it.
